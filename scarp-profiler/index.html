Scarp profiler

Extensional tectonics — throw and heave on a normal fault and its piedmont scarps

Teaching resources

Profile down dip (perpendicular to strike), from the footwall to the hanging wall. Throw is the vertical separation of those two surfaces. Heave = throw / tan(dip). Add every linked piedmont scarp to the range front; subtract antithetic scarps.

Why the profiles are drawn this way

Throw is how far the footwall surface has moved up relative to the hanging wall. Heave is the matching horizontal separation. For a planar fault, net slip = throw / sin(dip). A 60° normal fault with 10 m of throw has about 5.8 m of heave and 11.5 m of net slip. The dip defaults to 60° when you do not know it.

The profile has to run down dip. A line along the scarp mixes the scarp with the slope of the fan or the range front, and the vertical gap on that line is not the throw. Start on the upthrown block (usually the range) and end on the downthrown block (usually the basin).

A range-bounding normal fault often reaches the surface as the main range-front scarp plus one or more piedmont scarps on the bajada. Those scarps are the same fault system, stepped into the basin. Sum their throws, and sum their heaves. An antithetic scarp faces the other way, so its throw and heave count negative.

  1. Jump to a site, or click a Quaternary fault and choose Profile perpendicular to this fault here. You can also click the footwall, then the hanging wall, yourself.
  2. On the plot, mark the upper surface, the lower surface, and the scarp face. Each mark is two clicks. The tool fits a straight line through the elevations in between.
  3. Throw is the vertical gap between the upper and lower lines at the middle of the scarp. Set the dip. Heave and net slip update immediately.
  4. Repeat for each linked scarp. Leave checked the rows that belong to one fault system. The totals row sums them.
  5. Export a CSV or GeoJSON before you close the laptop. The browser also keeps this session on this computer.
